## Brand Overview

The brand

Hoea tō Waka provides resilience training, individual coaching and learning resources for organisations and the people who support others. The work is grounded in Aotearoa’s cultural context. Explain what the model helps people do before describing how it looks.

### Brand name

Hoea tō Waka

Write the name as three words: Hoea tō Waka. Keep the line above the ō in tō; this is a macron. Use this spelling in headings, documents and social posts.

### Brand line

Strength in Aotearoa

This short line sits with the name in the main logo. Keep the wording together when using that version of the logo.

### Vision

To see stronger, more resilient people, teams, and communities across Aotearoa.

The vision describes the future the work aims to contribute to.

### Mission

To deliver practical, culturally grounded resilience training, coaching, and resources that help people move through challenge with clarity and direction.

The mission describes what Hoea tō Waka does and how it supports people.

Our readers

## Who we speak to

The same person may be booking training for a team, looking for their own support or helping someone else. Write for what they need to decide, rather than assuming they know the model.

### People arranging training

HR staff, people leaders and managers need to know who a workshop is for, what it covers and how to enquire. Explain the format and how it can be tailored to their team.

### Leaders and team members

Someone considering individual coaching needs a clear picture of the conversation and the next step. Describe the practical focus without making them feel they should already have the answers.

### Support professionals

People working with clients, whānau and communities need to understand how the model can fit their practice. Use examples from their working context and explain specialist terms.

### People using resources

Readers, whānau, educators and workshop participants need to know what a book or video is about and how to get it. Make the title, purpose and order or viewing link easy to find.

## Favicon

A favicon is the small picture beside a website’s name in a browser tab. It also appears when someone saves the site as a bookmark. It helps people find Hoea tō Waka again among their open tabs and saved pages.

Use the symbol on its own, with a transparent background and a tight crop. At browser-tab sizes, every pixel matters: skip the surrounding tile, border and extra padding. ## Typography

Type system

Typography means the fonts and text styles we use. Inter is the main font for the Hoea tō Waka name, headings, paragraphs, menus and buttons. Use a heavier weight for headings so readers can quickly see what matters.

ABC Marist Book is the serif font used for testimonial quotes. Its letter shapes give people’s words a distinct voice. Keep the quote heading, person’s name and controls in Inter. The font specimens and usage guide show these roles using text from the site.

Regular 400, Semibold 600 and Book 350 name the font weights: how light or heavy the letters look. A serif font has small finishing strokes on its letters; Inter has simpler ends. Compare the samples to see the difference.

Inter 4.1: four static TTF styles, upright and italic variable TTFs, and two web WOFF2 files. Licence and installation notes included.

For office and design apps, choose Desktop/Static for Regular 400, Semibold 600 and their true italics. Choose Desktop/Variable instead if your app supports variable fonts; Web contains the WOFF2 versions. Use italics for short emphasis within text, and keep logo lettering upright. ABC Marist Book 350 remains the testimonial font.

Marist is licensed separately and isn’t included in the public pack.

### One clear visual language

#### Shape & stroke

Build on a 24 × 24 viewBox with no fill, rounded caps and rounded joins. Use a light, consistent outline; the examples use a 1.6-unit stroke.

#### Size & spacing

Use 18px for compact actions and 24px where more emphasis is needed. Keep the proportions intact and centre the artwork within its control. Small artwork still needs a generous hit area: aim for at least 44 × 44px for touch controls.

#### Colour & state

Let icons inherit the text colour. Use dark marks on light surfaces and light marks on dark surfaces, with enough contrast to stay visible. Pair success or error colours with a symbol and words.

#### Labels & meaning

Keep the same symbol for the same action. Hide decorative icons from screen readers; give an icon-only button a clear accessible name. Label unfamiliar actions in visible text rather than relying on hover.

## Section Spacing

Layout rhythm

Section spacing is the empty space above and below a block of content. It separates one topic from the next and stops the page feeling crowded. The current website uses the values below for standard sections; smaller screens use less space.

### How to apply it

For a standard website section, use 92px above and below on screens wider than 1100px, 65px from 761px to 1100px, and 60px at 760px or below. ## Colours

Choosing colour

Start with the current website: white page backgrounds, dark Ink text and black or white main buttons. Purple model sections and the coloured interior-page headers add variety. ### Main colour families

Ocean is blue, Forest is green, Aubergine is dark purple and Lavender is pale purple. Choose a colour for a clear purpose, such as separating a section or supporting an illustration. Keep the text easy to read against it.

### Lighter and darker shades

Each family runs from 100 (lightest) to 900 (darkest). The numbers are labels for choosing a shade, not percentages. Light shades can sit behind dark text; dark shades can sit behind white text. Check the contrast table before choosing a pair.

### Page backgrounds, text and borders

Paper is the white used for the main page. Ink is the dark colour used for text, main buttons and the footer. Stone is a light border colour. The remaining neutrals are available for supporting backgrounds and labels; they do not all need to appear in one design.

### Small supporting accents

Use these colours in small amounts when an illustration or diagram needs another colour. Keep the page, text and main action visually stronger than the accent so the reader knows where to look first.

### Colours that communicate a message

Success, Warning and Error tell people what happened or what needs their attention. Always include words or an icon as well as colour, so the meaning is not lost when someone cannot distinguish the colours. The focus colour shows where a keyboard user is on the page.

### Buttons: make the next step clear

The main website action is a rounded Ink button with white text and a white arrow circle. On a dark background, reverse it to white with dark text. Use a quieter outlined button for an alternative action. Labels should describe the action, such as “Explore services” or “Talk with Anna”.

#### On a light background

The arrow suggests moving to another page. A pause button or form control does not need this treatment.

#### On a dark background

Keep secondary actions readable without making them compete with the main button.

### Putting colours together

Use the white and Ink examples as the starting point for the website. Supporting backgrounds can separate longer topics or identify a section. These examples show individual colour choices; there is no fixed percentage of blue, green or purple to fill on a page.

These HEX values are for screens. For printed work, ask the printer to match the supplied artwork and approve a proof; the material and printing process affect the result.

### Can people read the text?

Contrast is the difference between the text and its background. A higher ratio means a stronger difference. Aim for at least 4.5:1 for body text and small labels. Large text can use 3:1: at least 24px in regular weight or about 18.7px in bold. The table checks the stricter body-text standard and updates when you edit a colour.

A “Needs attention” result means that pair should not be used for body text. Increase the difference between the two colours and check again. A passing colour pair still needs a readable font size and layout.

Contrast thresholds follow WCAG text contrast guidance. The table checks these examples, not every element on the website.

### Using and updating this guide

Use the examples to make consistent decisions, then check the result in the place people will see it: a phone, browser, printed card or workshop handout. Keep the name, logo and font roles consistent; let the amount of content determine the layout. When a brand decision changes, update this guide and its examples together.
